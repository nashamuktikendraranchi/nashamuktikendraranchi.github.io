Recognising Hidden Drinking at Home

Hidden drinking is common, especially when the family has already had one angry confrontation or when the person still goes out to work and needs to look steady in the morning. Concealment is not proof of evil character. It is often proof that the person both wants to keep using and wants to avoid shame. For relatives, the difficulty is to notice a pattern without turning the house into a police station.

Signs that are easy to explain away, one at a time

A bottle that is slightly less full than it was. Mouthwash used at odd hours. Long spells in the bathroom. "Work meetings" that leave the person smelling of mint and agitation. Cash missing in small amounts. Hidden packets in a scooter boot, a water tank platform, or a steel box that used to hold documents. A sudden interest in informing you, in detail, of every errand, which can be a way of controlling the story.

Any one of these can have an ordinary explanation. A cluster, lasting weeks, is different. Also notice morning tremor, broken sleep, skipping breakfast, or a short temper when plans change and the person cannot get out of the house. These are body signs, and they are harder to argue away than a missing cup of whisky.

How not to investigate

Searching every cupboard in a rage, photographing evidence for a family WhatsApp group, or staging a surprise inspection in front of children usually hardens denial. The person then becomes more skilled at hiding, and the household becomes more frightened. If you need to check, do it calmly, once, and then talk. Do not recruit a younger sibling as a spy. Do not use a child's testimony as a courtroom exhibit.

When you speak, describe what you have seen, not what you have concluded about their soul. "I found bottles behind the tank, and you have been shaking in the morning" is cleaner than "You have ruined this family." Ask whether they are ready to have a confidential conversation with a professional. Offer to come along. When concealment itself is a danger

Hidden drinking is especially risky when the person is also taking diabetes tablets, blood pressure medicine, sedatives, or painkillers, or when they drive. It is also risky when they have stopped openly and are now sipping in secret, because withdrawal and binge can sit close together. If you find the person confused, jaundiced, vomiting blood, or difficult to rouse, seek emergency care.
