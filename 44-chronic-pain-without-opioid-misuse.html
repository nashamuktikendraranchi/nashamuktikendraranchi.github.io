Managing Chronic Pain Without Sliding Back Into Opioid Misuse

Chronic pain is real. A bad back, an old injury, nerve pain, or joint disease does not disappear because a person has stopped misusing painkillers or other opioids. The fear, on one side, is of suffering without relief. The fear, on the other, is of a prescription that quietly becomes a daily need again. Both fears deserve respect. The way through is not toughness for its own sake, and not a return to unsupervised tablets "just until the pain settles."

Tell every clinician the recovery history

Before a new medicine is discussed, say that you have a history of opioid or other substance problems, and that you want the safest effective plan. Ask about physiotherapy, heat, suitable exercise, sleep, mood, and non-opioid medicines. If an opioid is truly being considered after a surgery or an acute injury, ask how long it will be used, who will hold it, and how it will be stopped. Do not keep leftover strips "in case."

If pain is also bound up with low mood, poor sleep, or heavy drinking in the past, those need attention too. Pain is not only a nerve signal. It is also a life with less movement, more frustration, and sometimes more isolation.

What families can do without becoming the pain police

Believe the pain. Help with ordinary supports: a walk that is short enough to be possible, a chair that does not make things worse, a clinic appointment that actually happens. Do not offer your own leftover painkillers. Do not shame the person for needing medical help. If you are worried about the way a medicine is being used, say so to the person and, if needed, to the prescribing doctor, rather than starting a secret nightly count that turns the house into a conflict zone.

A joint appointment can help when everyone is anxious and facts are getting lost.
