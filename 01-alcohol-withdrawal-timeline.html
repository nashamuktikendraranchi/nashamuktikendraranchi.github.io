Alcohol Withdrawal Timeline: What Families Usually See, Hour by Hour

When a heavy drinker suddenly stops, the body does not simply return to normal overnight. Alcohol has been damping down the nervous system. Once it is removed, that system can rebound: shaking, sweating, a racing heart, agitation, and in some people, seizures or confusion. Families in Jharkhand often hope the person will "sleep it off" in a locked room. A timeline will not predict every case, because health, age, how long the drinking has gone on, and earlier withdrawals all change the picture. It can, however, explain why the first three days are medically important.

The first 6 to 12 hours

Many people begin to feel unwell within a few hours of the last drink. Hands may tremble, sleep becomes shallow, and anxiety, sweating, nausea, or a headache are common. The person may ask for "just one drink to settle the nerves." That request is a withdrawal signal, not proof that a small dose is harmless. If someone already has a history of morning drinking, these early signs can start sooner.

This is the point at which a family should stop treating the episode as a hangover. Offer water, a quiet room, and company, but do not leave the person alone if they are very restless, vomiting, or confused. The next day, and the risk of seizures

Between about 12 and 48 hours, symptoms can intensify. Blood pressure and pulse may rise. The person may be irritable, frightened, or unable to sit still. In some people who have been drinking heavily for a long time, a seizure can occur in this window, sometimes without a long warning. A seizure at home is a medical emergency. Do not put objects in the mouth, and seek emergency help.

People who have withdrawn before, who also use other sedating medicines, or who have liver disease, head injury, or poor nutrition, may be at higher risk. Families cannot safely judge that risk from the doorway. Two to three days, and the danger of confusion

From roughly 48 to 72 hours, some severely dependent people develop delirium tremens: confusion, severe agitation, hallucinations, fever, and dangerous changes in pulse or blood pressure. It does not happen to everyone, and it does not always arrive on a tidy schedule, but it is a reason not to gamble on a locked bedroom. Delirium tremens is a medical emergency, not a character problem.

After the most intense days, sleep, mood, and appetite can remain unsettled. That later stretch is not usually as immediately dangerous as the first three days, but it still needs food, rest, and a plan, not a sudden return to the same drinking circle.

What a family should do with this timeline

Use it as a reason to get advice early, not as a chart to manage withdrawal yourself. Do not taper alcohol at home by guesswork, and do not add leftover sleeping tablets or painkillers unless a doctor has said so.
